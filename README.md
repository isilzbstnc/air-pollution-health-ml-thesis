# Predicting the Health Impact of Air Pollution with Machine Learning

My undergraduate thesis in Statistics at Yıldız Technical University (2026), supervised by Prof. Dr. Serpil Kılıç Depren. The full thesis is in Turkish: [thesis.pdf](thesis.pdf)

The question: how well can different models predict a health impact score from air pollution, weather and hospital case data, and which variables drive the prediction? I trained seven models on the same data and compared them on the same held-out test set.

> **Note on the data:** The dataset is synthetic. Its values were generated by the dataset author, not measured. The results below compare methods; they are not findings about real public health.

## Data

[Air Quality and Health Impact Dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/rabieelkharoua/air-quality-and-health-impact-dataset) by Rabie El Kharoua (Kaggle, 2024), 5,811 rows. The data is not included in this repository; download it from Kaggle.

| Group | Variables |
|---|---|
| Air pollutants | AQI, PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, O3 |
| Weather | Temperature, Humidity, WindSpeed |
| Health indicators | RespiratoryCases, CardiovascularCases, HospitalAdmissions |
| Target | HealthImpactScore (0 to 100) |

`HealthImpactClass` was dropped because it is derived directly from the target score and would leak it into the models.

## Method

All work was done in R (RStudio).

- One 80/20 train/test split, identical for every model
- Linear models: multiple linear regression, Lasso and Ridge (penalty chosen with k-fold cross-validation)
- Machine learning models: KNN (standardized inputs, k = 11 chosen with 5-fold cross-validation), Random Forest (500 trees, 4 variables tried at each split), SVR and XGBoost (learning rate 0.05, max depth 6, 1,000 rounds)
- Evaluation on the test set with R², RMSE and MAE
- Residual analysis and VIF for the linear model, SHAP values to explain XGBoost
- For the two best models: a check of log transformation and standardization, then a scenario-based sensitivity analysis

## Results

| Model | R² | RMSE | MAE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multiple linear regression | 0.4999 | 9.49 | 7.12 |
| Lasso | 0.4805 | 9.37 | 7.40 |
| Ridge | 0.4800 | 9.36 | 7.30 |
| KNN | 0.7602 | 7.82 | 4.02 |
| Random Forest | 0.9467 | 3.50 | 1.89 |
| SVR | 0.9538 | 2.92 | 2.18 |
| **XGBoost** | **0.9710** | **2.48** | **1.42** |

What stood out:

- The linear models stop at an R² of about 0.50. The target is capped at 100 and many rows sit exactly at that cap. The residuals form a clear V shape, so a straight line cannot follow the data.
- Tree-based models and SVR handle this non-linearity well. XGBoost had the best score on all three metrics.
- AQI is the most important variable by a wide margin in both Random Forest and XGBoost (SHAP), followed by particulate matter and the other pollutants. Weather and case-count variables add very little.
- Log transformation and standardization did not improve the two best models. Log transformation made SVR slightly worse (R² 0.9315).

### Scenario analysis

Change in the predicted health impact score:

| Scenario | XGBoost | SVR |
|---|---|---|
| Pollutants −20% | −5.47 | −5.98 |
| Pollutants +10% | +1.61 | +1.75 |
| Weather change (temperature +10%, humidity −10%, wind speed −20%) | −0.11 | +0.01 |

Both models agree on the direction for pollutants, and the weather scenario barely moves the score. At the disease level (respiratory and cardiovascular cases) the two models often disagreed, which fits how little signal the case counts carry in this dataset.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data.** The pollutant and weather variables are close to uniformly distributed (kurtosis around −1.2 for each, means in the middle of each range), and the case counts behave like Poisson draws (variance close to the mean). Real pollution data is usually skewed and correlated. The high R² values mainly show that the models can recover the rule used to generate the score.
- **No causal claims.** The models find associations in the data. The scenarios show how the predictions respond, not what would happen to real people.
- **No time dimension.** Lagged effects of exposure, which matter for respiratory disease, could not be studied.
